03 / Context

Forms are gateways to essential services

Forms are essential to healthcare, finance, education, and everyday life. Yet many form experiences remain inaccessible to blind and low-vision users.

  • Loss of independence
  • Exposure of private information
  • Increased risk of input errors
  • Reduced confidence
  • Dependence on sighted assistance
Central problem

“Poorly designed forms can force blind and low-vision users to rely on sighted assistance, compromising their independence, privacy, and confidence.”

Research question

How might we design form experiences that allow blind and low-vision users to complete forms independently and securely?

06 / Research analysis

Making patterns visible

Interview data was analyzed through affinity mapping in FigJam, thematic clustering of participant experiences and quotes, identification of repeated barriers, and priority mapping based on user impact and frequency. Findings were then translated into design recommendations.

07 / Key insights

Five barriers—and the opportunities they reveal

01

Paper Forms Eliminate Independence

Paper forms almost always require sighted assistance. This can expose sensitive medical, financial, or personal information. Even braille-based solutions have limitations when staff members or other people cannot interpret braille.

Design opportunity

Provide accessible digital alternatives and private assistance options for every paper-only process.

02

Digital Forms Still Break Screen Readers

Missing, unclear, or incorrect semantic labels can leave users unsure what information belongs in a field, causing information to be entered in the wrong place.

Design opportunity

Use meaningful labels, logical reading order, clear field instructions, and properly implemented semantic structure.

03

Formatting and Input Rules Are Unclear

Participants may be unable to determine whether checkboxes are selected or what format a field requires. This creates repeated errors and frustration.

Design opportunity

Communicate selection states and formatting requirements before users submit their information.

04

Error Feedback Is Vague

Error messages often fail to identify the affected field or explain how to correct it. Users may have to navigate through the entire form again.

Design opportunity

Connect each error to its field, explain the issue clearly, and provide specific correction guidance.

05

AI Support Is Promising, but Trust Is Critical

Participants expressed interest in AI-assisted form completion and concerns about privacy, data security, accuracy, and transparency. Vetted human assistance services such as Aira may feel more trustworthy but can be expensive.

Design opportunity

Build AI assistance around informed consent, transparency, user control, and strong privacy protections.

08 / Design recommendations

An accessibility-first framework

  • Apply proper semantic labeling to every field, checkbox, button, and instruction.
  • Provide descriptive, field-specific error messages with correction guidance.
  • Offer accessible digital alternatives for paper-dependent processes.
  • Clearly communicate input formats, required fields, and selection states.
  • Use AI guidance to explain fields, clarify instructions, and read inputs back.
  • Allow users to review, confirm, or correct information before submission.
  • Combine secure technology with access to vetted professionals when needed.
  • Center transparency, consent, privacy, and user control in AI support.

10 / Reflection

Accessibility is about dignity, not only compliance

This project showed me that accessible form design is not only about technical compliance. It is about enabling people to complete essential tasks with independence, dignity, privacy, and confidence. Effective accessibility research requires listening closely to users of assistive technologies and translating their experiences into requirements that design and development teams can act on.
