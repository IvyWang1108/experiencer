01 / Overview

Researching more than whether information can be found

This project explored what motivates a search, how people describe symptoms, which tools and sources they use, how credibility and tone shape responses, when searching stops, and what helps someone decide what to do next.

My responsibilities

  • Planned and conducted contextual observations

  • Built an inductive codebook and affinity map

03 / Context & problem

Searching while already uncertain

Online health information can be overwhelming, inconsistent, difficult to evaluate, and emotionally alarming. Non-medical users may encounter conflicting explanations, technical terminology, alarmist language, unclear credibility, irrelevant results, and limited next-step guidance.

Scope: This study examined information-seeking behavior. It did not provide diagnoses or replace professional healthcare.

Problem statement

“People frequently turn to online search for health concerns, but the information they encounter can be overwhelming, inconsistent, and anxiety-inducing.”

Research question

How might we help non-medical users feel confident, informed, and safe when searching for health information online?

  • How do users describe and search for symptoms?
  • What signals make information appear trustworthy?
  • What helps users decide on an appropriate next step?

05 / Research approach

Two complementary qualitative phases

  1. Frame
  2. Observe
  3. Interview
  4. Analyze
  5. Synthesize
  6. Recommend
Phase 1

Contextual observations

Eight sessions with participants ages 21–32 and no medical background. Participants completed a live symptom-search task using think-aloud.

Observed

Everyday language, navigation, platforms, scanning, source choices, credibility cues, emotions, and stopping points.

Analysis

AEIOU framework, affinity mapping, sequence-flow analysis.

Phase 2

In-depth interviews

Eight sessions with participants ages 21–32 and no medical background. Semi-structured interviews lasted approximately 30–45 minutes.

Topics

Past searches, credibility, emotions, preferences, decisions, source comparison, AI, and professional care.

Analysis

Inductive coding, codebook creation, affinity diagramming, thematic synthesis.

These were two groups of eight research sessions; the available information does not establish that all 16 sessions involved unique participants.

06 / Why two methods?

Behavior plus reasoning

Observation revealed what users did

How they phrased searches, clicked, scanned, moved between sources, and stopped.

Interviews revealed why

What felt trustworthy, how information felt, what reassurance meant, and how action was chosen.

“Observation revealed behavior, while interviews revealed the reasoning and emotions behind that behavior.”

08 / Key findings

What shapes confidence and action

01

Users search for reassurance, not a diagnosis

The immediate question was often, “Should I be worried?” Many stopped after feeling sufficiently reassured rather than identifying an exact condition.

Why it matters

More information does not always create confidence; understandable severity and next-step guidance matter.

Design opportunity

Prioritize calm, actionable urgency guidance without presenting a definitive diagnosis.

02

Search behavior is visual and nonlinear

Participants used everyday language, multiple tabs, image comparison, summaries, and revised queries.

Why it matters

Linear pages may not match how users assemble understanding across snippets and sources.

Design opportunity

Support visual comparison, plain-language queries, concise summaries, and flexible movement.

03

Trust relies on surface-level signals

Familiar brands, .org domains, repeated information, and professional presentation commonly shaped credibility. Few consistently investigated authorship, citations, or evidence.

Why it matters

Information can appear credible without its quality being verified.

Design opportunity

Make medical review, verification, authorship, and review dates visible and understandable.

04

Tone shapes continued engagement

Alarmist language could increase anxiety or cause disengagement; calm, structured information encouraged trust and attention.

Why it matters

Tone influences emotional safety, trust, and whether information is reviewed.

Design opportunity

Explain possible severity with calm, balanced, non-sensational language.

09 / Behavioral personas

Different strategies, shared needs

These personas come from behavioral spectrums—not rigid demographic categories.

Riya

The Visualizer

Core behavior: Uses visual information for rapid symptom comparison and reassurance.

  • Image search and visual matching
  • Concise, actionable information
  • Scans and may stop after reassurance
  • May choose self-treatment

Needs

Visual comparisons, quick summaries, plain language, warning signs, next steps.

Potential risk

Visual similarity may create confidence without enough context.

Rami

The Researcher

Core behavior: Builds confidence through deep reading and source comparison.

  • Established institutions and cross-checking
  • Detailed explanations
  • AI for interpreting complexity
  • More likely to consult a professional

Needs

Source details, credibility indicators, plain language, comparison, escalation guidance.

Potential risk

Information volume may prolong uncertainty.

Both need calm language, understandable credibility cues, and actionable next steps.

12 / Design recommendations

Five proposed opportunities

01

Visual symptom matching

Image-supported comparison with plain-language context, multiple examples, visible warning signs, and explicit non-diagnostic framing.

02

Credibility indicators

Explain doctor review, verification, authorship, dates, and supporting sources—not decorative badges.

03

Quick-read summaries

Key points, common signs, warnings, next steps, and optional detail.

04

AI-assisted explanations

Explain terms, summarize, clarify, and compare while disclosing that AI can be incomplete or incorrect and does not replace medical advice.

05

Clear escalation cues

Condition-specific, professionally reviewed care guidance. “If symptoms persist for three or more days, consult a doctor” is only an example of the guidance participants wanted—not universal medical advice.

13 / Proposed design direction · Not tested or completed

One framework, two entry paths

  1. Describe symptom
  2. Review visual + text information
  3. Check credibility
  4. Read plain-language summary
  5. Understand warning signs
  6. Choose an informed next step

Riya can begin with images and quick summaries.

Rami can continue into detail and source information.

Both receive calm, visible next-step guidance.

14 / Ethical & safety considerations

Reassurance cannot replace accurate risk communication

  • Never present results as diagnosis
  • Avoid false reassurance
  • Require medical review
  • Explain AI limitations
  • Protect symptom and search privacy
  • Avoid sensational language
  • Make urgent-care guidance findable
  • Separate information from medical advice
  • Design for accessibility and varied health literacy
15 / Limitations & future research

What this study can—and cannot—support

Confirmed limitations

  • Participants were ages 21–32
  • All had no medical background
  • Scope was online search behavior
  • Findings should not generalize to every population, condition, or context

Future opportunities

  • Broaden ages and health-literacy levels
  • Study accessibility needs
  • Compare search, AI, and health platforms
  • Test credibility indicators and summaries
  • Study visual matching and confidence
  • Validate content with healthcare professionals
16 / Final reflection

Searching for health information is both informational and emotional.

Users are not only asking what a symptom might mean—they are also trying to understand whether they should be worried and what they should do next.

By combining contextual observation with in-depth interviews, I connected visible search behavior with the trust, uncertainty, and decision-making behind it. The project strengthened my ability to translate complex qualitative findings into personas and actionable design direction while remaining attentive to the safety and responsibility required in healthcare UX.
