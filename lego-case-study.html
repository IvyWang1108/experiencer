Improving Usability Across the LEGO E-Commerce Experience

Evaluating how users shop, discover products, create wish lists, and build with individual bricks on LEGO.com.

01 / Overview

Evaluating an established shopping experience

This academic usability evaluation examined shopping and product discovery on LEGO.com: finding and saving products, searching for individual pieces, understanding distinct bag systems, browsing with themes and filters, and completing common shopping tasks with confidence.

My responsibilities

  • Helped design task scenarios and usability-test scripts
  • Facilitated and observed moderated sessions
  • Guided participants through a think-aloud protocol
  • Recorded issues, behaviors, and participant quotes
  • Analyzed task success and user difficulty
  • Synthesized findings into themes
  • Developed prioritized design recommendations

03 / Context & problem

A rich catalog with uneven interaction patterns

LEGO.com supports shopping behaviors ranging from purchasing complete sets to selecting individual bricks. Users completed targeted product-shopping tasks, but encountered serious difficulties with wish lists and Pick-a-Brick. The evaluation focused on identifying where current patterns support users and where they break down—not redesigning the entire site.

Evaluation scope

Shopping, saving products, browsing, filtering, and selecting individual bricks.

Central problem

“Despite LEGO’s strong brand and extensive product catalog, users can struggle to complete common shopping tasks because of confusing navigation, inconsistent interface elements, and limited system feedback.”

Research question

How might we improve task success and user confidence when people shop, save products, and build with individual bricks on LEGO.com?

05 / Research approach

Testing realistic tasks remotely

  1. Plan

Participants

Five participants with varied LEGO familiarity and e-commerce experience.

Format & environment

Moderated remote sessions over Zoom. Participants used their own devices for a more natural shopping experience.

Protocol

Participants completed realistic tasks while thinking aloud. Actions, expectations, difficulties, and comments were recorded for analysis.

Three shopping scenarios

Evaluated

Task 02

Use Pick-a-Brick to find the pieces needed to build a car.

Evaluated
  • Search, filtering, and brick selection
  • Bricks Bag versus Shopping Bag
  • Confirmation and feedback
Task 03

Find an architecture gift within budget

Browse for an architecture-themed gift within a set budget.

Evaluated
  • Category navigation and filters
  • Product discovery
  • Decision-making confidence

09 / Key findings

Where users struggled—and succeeded

01

The wish list is difficult to discover

Two of five participants initially failed. The heart was interpreted as a “like” button, and participants expected multiple named lists instead of a default “General” list.

“I thought the heart icon was just a like button.”
Design opportunity

Make the wish list’s purpose visible and support more flexible organization.

02

The Pick-a-Brick flow completely breaks down

None of five participants succeeded. Search results were inaccurate or empty, filters were insufficient, and the distinction between the Bricks Bag and Shopping Bag was unclear.

“I thought I added it already—I did the same thing as adding a set.”
Design opportunity

Make Pick-a-Brick feel like a guided building and shopping tool instead of a separate, unfamiliar system.

03

Targeted product shopping works well

All five participants found an architecture-themed gift within budget. Existing filters and category navigation supported confident decisions.

Design opportunity

Reuse successful main-shopping patterns in weaker flows, particularly Pick-a-Brick.

11 / Prioritized recommendations

Repair the critical flow first

Priority 1 · Critical

Repair the Pick-a-Brick flow

  • Improve keyword matching, accuracy, autocomplete, and suggested categories.
  • Direct individual-part searches to Pick-a-Brick.
  • Add filters for size, shape, transparency, and material.
  • Provide inline guidance and explicit add-to-bag confirmation.
  • Explain and visually distinguish the two bag systems.
Priority 2

Clarify the wish list

  • Add “Save to Wish List” beside the heart.
  • Keep the action consistent across pages.
  • Support named lists and clear confirmation.
Priority 3

Extend successful patterns

  • Preserve effective themed-shopping patterns.
  • Apply familiar behaviors to Pick-a-Brick.
  • Keep navigation, feedback, and terminology consistent.
12 / Reflection

Test assumptions, then compare breakdowns with success

“This evaluation showed that an existing interface pattern should not be assumed to be effective simply because it is already part of the product. Research helps teams test those assumptions, refine content, improve categorization, and identify where familiar interactions no longer match users’ expectations.”

“The strongest design opportunities came from comparing where users struggled with where they succeeded. LEGO.com already contains effective shopping patterns—the opportunity is to apply those patterns more consistently across the entire experience.”
